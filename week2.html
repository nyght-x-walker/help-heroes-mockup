Five People, Five Angles

A wicked problem eats single-discipline teams. This one mixes UX, learning design, community roots, data, and ethics. Deep in one craft each, fluent across the rest: T-shaped.

Redesign the online learning experience for first-generation university students in a world in which access to high-speed internet is unreliable.

Why this mix

UX plus learning plus community plus data plus ethics. Five countries against a narrow Western lens. Five crafts, one brief, deliberate friction.

Productive tension

Everyone pulls toward the same center, in different directions. That is the point.

First impressions, in their own words

Linh: start from the day, not the spec

"We need to prioritize empathy with first-generation students by understanding their daily realities: internet dropping during lectures, sharing devices with family members, and the anxiety of being left behind. Then design simple, low-bandwidth solutions that can work offline and sync when connection returns."

Keisha: redesign the activity, not just the file

"Our focus should be on reimagining learning activities themselves. Not just making videos downloadable, but designing asynchronous, text-based, low-bandwidth learning experiences that maintain engagement and social connection even without constant connectivity."

Carlos: work inside the constraints

"We must involve students and their communities from the start, recognizing that unreliable internet is often a symptom of deeper infrastructure and equity issues. Our solution needs to work within existing constraints rather than assuming those constraints will disappear."

Rajiv: small AI, honest AI

"We can leverage lightweight AI models that operate on limited data to personalize learning pathways, predict when connectivity will be available, and optimize content delivery based on bandwidth availability. But only if we design with fairness and transparency in mind."

Tobias: interrogate the goal first

"Before we design anything, we need to critically examine the underlying assumptions: What do we mean by 'successful learning'? Whose definition of educational quality are we using? And could our 'solution' inadvertently create new forms of exclusion or dependence that we haven't considered?"
