## Task 2: Process Reflection

### I Liked:

What parts did I particularly enjoy?  
Looking at the whole seven weeks, I enjoyed the early observation and synthesis phases more than I expected. Sorting through user data and gradually identifying a real problem statement was intellectually engaging. But the testing phase was the most instructive. It gave me a clear check on whether the earlier work actually made sense. Watching users interact with the prototype connected the abstract synthesis directly to concrete reactions. That moment-when an early insight either held up or collapsed-made the whole process feel coherent.

What positively surprised me?  
I was surprised that trusting the process actually worked. I had doubted the amount of time spent on observation before moving to solutions. But that investment made the later phases much more focused. I also did not expect that the shift from observation to prototyping would feel as natural as it did. The ideas that emerged in Weeks 4 and 5 were clearly built on the groundwork laid earlier, not pulled from nowhere.

What areas would I like to work on?  
I want to improve my prototyping skills, particularly in creating higher-fidelity versions earlier. Low-fidelity prototypes are useful, but more polished ones would have drawn out better feedback from testers. I also need to work on facilitation. Managing a design thinking workshop requires a different approach than just taking part, and I found that harder than I thought.

-

### I Found Challenging:

What parts did I find difficult?  
The ideation phase was harder than I anticipated. I kept falling back on familiar solutions instead of generating genuinely different options. This matches what Lewrick et al. describe about stakeholders being stuck in older mindsets-I realised I was doing the same thing myself.

What negatively surprised me?  
I did not expect the level of pushback from other departments. Technology questioned the feasibility. Marketing had different priorities. Legal raised concerns at the first prototype. Management challenged the timeline. The “not invented here” attitude mentioned in the readings appeared clearly in practice, and it slowed things down.

What would I like to work on?  
I should develop better stakeholder engagement strategies, and I should start them earlier. The reading on stakeholder maps stood out to me. In hindsight, I should have identified key stakeholders in Week 2 or 3 and thought through their positions-for example, what the CFO would care about, or who might block the project and why.

The implementation gap also concerned me. The HPI and Stanford finding-that design thinking does not consistently increase the number of launched products-matched my experience. Norman's point that most radical ideas take decades to succeed felt relevant even at this smaller scale.

-

### I Learnt:

About the design challenge:  
I learned that the initial problem was only a surface symptom. Through observation and synthesis, I found that users were describing a frustration that covered a deeper need. The project reinforced the importance of root-cause analysis, not just accepting user complaints at face value.

About the users:  
Users are good at describing what bothers them, but they are often poor at describing what they actually need or how to solve it. Observation gave me more useful data than direct questioning. I also learned that different user groups have different priorities, and what works for one can fail for another.

About design thinking methods and mindsets:  
Several points stood out. First, iteration works. Each round of testing produced useful changes, which matches Norman’s view that slow, steady improvements accumulate. Second, stakeholder involvement matters. The stakeholder map method from the reading-using physical objects to map relationships-would have helped me avoid some of the resistance I encountered. Third, the tension between exploration and exploitation is real. Management pushed for short-term results while the process called for longer-term thinking. Fourth, implementation is not automatic. As Lewrick et al. note, many ideas never reach the market, and I now see why.

About myself:  
I noticed a tendency to settle for the first workable solution rather than exploring widely. Norman’s hill-climbing metaphor fits my approach-I tended to climb the nearest peak instead of searching for higher ground. I also became more comfortable with uncertainty over the weeks. I had wanted clear direction early on, but the open-ended phases eventually made sense. I also saw that I need to communicate differently with different stakeholders, tailoring the message to their concerns rather than giving everyone the same explanation.

-

### Reflection on Implementation

The readings help explain why implementation is so difficult. Traditional organisations focus on stability and predictability, as Lewrick et al. describe. Design thinking needs experimentation and iteration. These two orientations do not align easily, and that mismatch showed up in the resistance I encountered.

The stakeholder map approach would have been useful. The reading suggests asking what each stakeholder stands to gain or lose. I should have done that earlier. Norman’s videophone example-over a century from concept to use-also made me think more carefully about the time and persistence required to move from a tested idea to actual adoption.

-

### Looking Forward

This project changed how I approach problem-solving. I will start with observation and stay longer in the problem space before moving to solutions. I will prototype earlier, even if crudely, and use feedback to adjust. I will also plan for organisational resistance from the beginning, not treat it as an afterthought.

As Norman writes, good design is threatened by unnecessary features and short-term thinking. Design thinking offers a way to stay focused on real human needs, but that only matters if we can get the solution into use. That is the part I will pay more attention to going forward.
